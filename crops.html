      Planting Start Methods Explained

      ×
      Direct Sow
      Seeds are planted directly into the soil where the plants will grow. This method is simple and works best for crops that develop quickly or don’t transplant well.
      Example: Carrots, beans, corn.
      Start in Trays, Transplant in Ground
      Seeds are first sown in trays or seedbeds indoors or in a nursery, and once seedlings are strong enough, they are transplanted to the main field or garden.
      Example: Tomatoes, peppers, cabbage.
      Transplant
      Young plants grown elsewhere (nursery or greenhouse) are moved and planted in their final growing location. This helps ensure strong plants and better survival rates.
      Example: Lettuce, broccoli, cauliflower.
      Container
      Plants are grown entirely in pots or containers rather than in the ground. This is useful for small spaces, patios, or areas with poor soil.
      Example: Herbs, small vegetables, strawberries.
      Grown in Trays
      Seeds are sown in trays and grown until seedlings are ready. This is similar to starting in trays, but may stay in trays longer or be sold directly from trays.
      Example: Microgreens, leafy seedlings, nursery crops.
      Root Stock
      A healthy root system used as the base for grafting or propagation of other plants. Often used in fruit trees to improve disease resistance and growth.
      Example: Apple trees, citrus trees.
      Bulbs
      Plants are grown from underground storage organs like bulbs, corms, or tubers. These naturally contain nutrients to start growth.
      Example: Onions, tulips, garlic, potatoes.
      Grafting
      A method of joining two plant parts so that they grow as one. Typically used to combine a strong rootstock with a desired fruiting variety.
      Example: Mango, apple, citrus trees.
      Layering
      A branch or stem is bent to the ground and covered with soil to grow roots while still attached to the parent plant. Once rooted, it can be cut and planted independently.
      Example: Strawberries, blackberries, some shrubs.
      Division
      Clumping plants are divided into smaller sections, each with roots and shoots, and planted separately. This is a simple way to propagate perennials.
      Example: Hostas, daylilies, ornamental grasses.

      Soil Conditions Explained

      ×
      Loamy
      Fertile, well-drained soil that retains moisture. Ideal for most crops and easy to work with.
      Example: Tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers.
      Sandy
      Drains quickly and warms fast, but low in nutrients. Requires frequent watering and fertilization.
      Example: Carrots, radishes, onions.
      Clay
      Heavy soil that retains water and nutrients well, but drains slowly and can compact easily.
      Example: Broccoli, cabbage, tomatoes (with raised beds).
      Silty
      Smooth, fertile soil that holds moisture well and is easy to till, but may compact if wet.
      Example: Leafy greens, beans, corn.
      Peaty
      Acidic soil high in organic matter and moisture. Good for certain vegetables but may need lime to reduce acidity.
      Example: Strawberries, blueberries, lettuce.
      Chalky
      Alkaline, stony soil that drains quickly and can be low in nutrients. May need fertilization or soil amendment.
      Example: Cabbage, cauliflower, spinach.
